Hurricane Season Plumbing Checklist for Fort Lauderdale Homeowners

A little plumbing-specific prep before hurricane season peaks in September can prevent a minor storm from becoming a major water damage claim.

Atlantic hurricane season runs officially from June 1 through November 30 each year, according to NOAA's National Hurricane Center, with the season's climatological peak falling around September 10. For Fort Lauderdale homeowners, that six-month window is also when plumbing problems tend to spike, driven by storm surge, heavy rainfall backing up into canal-adjacent properties, and power outages that can take sump pumps and well systems offline at the worst possible moment.

A little plumbing-specific prep before the season ramps up can be the difference between a minor inconvenience and a major water damage claim. Here's what we tell our own customers to check.

The Role Of Broward County's Canal System

Fort Lauderdale earned its nickname, the Venice of America, from its extensive network of residential canals, and that same waterway system plays a real role in how the city handles heavy rain during storm season. Canal-adjacent properties sit at a different elevation and drainage profile than inland homes, and during a major rain event, water levels in the canal system can rise faster than storm drains can keep pace with. That backup pressure is part of why backflow prevention matters more for a canal-front property than for a home several miles inland.

If your property borders a canal or sits at a low point relative to the street, it's worth having a plumber assess your specific backflow and drainage setup before the season peaks, rather than discovering a gap in that protection during an actual storm.

Generator And Power Outage Considerations

A prolonged power outage is one of the most overlooked plumbing risks during hurricane season. A sump pump, well pump, or septic system's effluent pump all depend on electricity, and a multi-day outage can leave a property vulnerable exactly when heavy rain is most likely to be testing the drainage system. If your property relies on any pump for plumbing function, confirm whether it has battery backup, and if you have a generator, confirm it's rated to run that specific equipment before the season starts rather than during an active outage.

Before The Season Starts

Know Your Main Water Shutoff

Locate it now, not during an emergency. If a pipe fails during a storm, being able to shut off water fast limits the damage significantly.

Check Your Sump Pump

If your property has one, test it and confirm you have a battery or generator backup in case of a power outage during the storm.

Inspect Backflow Devices

A functioning backflow preventer keeps storm-driven floodwater from being pushed back into your home's plumbing system.

Have A Plumber's Number Ready

Save an emergency plumbing number in your phone before you need it, not while you're standing in rising water.

What Storm Surge And Heavy Rain Actually Do To Plumbing

Fort Lauderdale's extensive canal network is part of what makes the city distinctive. It also means heavy rain events can push water back into low-elevation properties faster than in cities without that kind of waterway density. Storm surge from a hurricane can overwhelm municipal drainage and, in the worst cases, force water back through exterior drain lines and even toilets in ground-level bathrooms.

Sewage backup during a major storm event is also a real risk, particularly in older neighborhoods where sewer lines are already dealing with age and root intrusion before a storm adds extra strain. A backflow valve on the main sewer line, where a property allows for one, is worth discussing with a licensed plumber well before storm season, not during it.

What Happens If You Wait

Waiting out a season without a plumbing checkup is a gamble more homeowners lose than expect. A backflow device that's been quietly failing for a year, a sump pump battery that's been dead since last season, or a main shutoff valve that's seized from disuse are all things that only become obvious at the worst possible moment. That moment is usually mid-storm, with water already coming in. None of these take more than an hour for a technician to check and, if needed, fix, and doing it in the calm of May costs far less in every sense than discovering the problem in September.

During And After The Storm

Shut Off Water If Flooding Occurs

If water is entering the home, shutting off the main valve limits how much additional water your own plumbing can contribute.

Avoid Contaminated Water

Storm surge and sewage backup water should be treated as contaminated. Don't wade through it if you can avoid it.

Check For Damage Once Safe

Once conditions allow, inspect visible plumbing for cracks, leaks, or damage from wind-blown debris.

Call For Mitigation Fast

If water got into the home, fast mitigation limits how much of your home ends up needing full restoration.

FAQs

Should I turn off my water heater before a hurricane?

If you're evacuating for an extended period, turning off both the water supply and power to an electric water heater is a reasonable precaution. Gas units should generally be left as configured unless you're told otherwise by your utility.

Can a plumber actually do anything once a hurricane is already happening?

Response during active severe weather depends on safe road conditions. Once it's safe to travel, emergency response resumes as quickly as possible.

Does homeowner's insurance cover hurricane-related plumbing damage?

Sudden damage from wind or storm surge is often covered, though flood damage specifically may require separate flood insurance. Check your specific policy well before storm season.
