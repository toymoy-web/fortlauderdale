Grease Trap Compliance for Fort Lauderdale Restaurants

FOG compliance isn't optional for a commercial kitchen. Here's where the requirements come from and how to build a maintenance plan that actually holds up to inspection.

Any restaurant, cafe, or commercial kitchen in Fort Lauderdale is producing wastewater loaded with fats, oils, and grease, commonly shortened to FOG in the industry. What happens to that FOG before it reaches the sewer system is a real compliance question, not an optional detail, and getting it wrong can mean fines, a shutdown order, or a sewer backup that closes your kitchen for days.

Where The Requirement Actually Comes From

The EPA doesn't issue a direct grease trap mandate for individual restaurants. Instead, its Clean Water Act pretreatment program, found at 40 CFR Part 403, authorizes municipalities operating public wastewater treatment facilities to set their own local FOG requirements. That means the specific rules that apply to a Fort Lauderdale kitchen come from the local utility and municipal code, not a single national standard. Those specifics are worth confirming directly with your local public works or pretreatment coordinator rather than assuming a rule from another city applies here.

What A Grease Trap Actually Does

Captures FOG Before The Sewer

A grease interceptor slows wastewater flow long enough for fats and oils to separate and float, keeping them out of the sewer line.

Protects The Line, Not Just The Kitchen

Grease that reaches the sewer hardens as it cools, narrowing the pipe over time and eventually causing a backup that can affect neighboring properties too.

Sized To Your Kitchen's Volume

A trap sized for a small cafe won't handle a high-volume kitchen's output, and undersizing is a common reason for repeat maintenance issues.

Subject To A Maintenance Schedule

Most jurisdictions require documented pumping and cleaning on a set schedule, not just when a problem shows up.

Common Compliance Mistakes

Skipping Documentation

Even if the trap is being serviced, missing maintenance records can result in a violation during an inspection.

Undersizing For Actual Volume

Kitchen expansion or a menu change that increases fryer or grill volume can outgrow an existing trap's capacity.

Inconsistent Pumping Schedule

An irregular schedule based on convenience rather than actual buildup rate leads to more emergency backups, not fewer.

Ignoring Employee Practices

Pouring grease down a drain instead of into designated collection containers adds load the trap wasn't sized to handle.

What Happens If You're Found Non-Compliant

Enforcement varies significantly by jurisdiction, but consequences for FOG violations commonly include fines and mandatory corrective action within a set timeframe. In repeat or severe cases, a suspension of your ability to discharge wastewater can follow, which functionally means a forced closure until the issue is resolved. A backup that spills into a dining area or, worse, a neighboring business, adds cleanup costs and potential liability well beyond the compliance fine itself.

What To Do If A Grease Trap Backs Up During Service

Stop Using Affected Drains Immediately

Continuing to run water into a backed-up line only adds to the mess and can push contaminated water further into your kitchen.

Clear The Area For Safety

A grease backup creates a genuine slip hazard on kitchen floors. Block off the area until it's cleaned and dried.

Don't Attempt A DIY Fix Mid-Service

Pouring chemicals or attempting to clear the line yourself during business hours risks making the backup worse and delays a proper repair.

Call For Emergency Service

A backup during service hours is a revenue-impacting emergency, and prioritizing a fast professional response limits how long you're closed.

Building A Realistic Maintenance Plan

Rather than waiting for an inspection or an obvious backup to prompt action, most well-run commercial kitchens set up a recurring service schedule matched to their actual volume, confirmed against local requirements. This typically means periodic pumping by a licensed hauler, documented in a manifest your business can produce on request, alongside routine jetting of the connected kitchen lines to keep grease from accumulating in the pipe itself between trap services.

Yellow Grease Versus Brown Grease

Not all kitchen grease is handled the same way. Used cooking oil, often called yellow grease, is typically collected separately from the grease trap itself and can actually generate revenue for a kitchen through a recycling or rendering service, rather than being a pure disposal cost. Brown grease, the material captured inside the trap or interceptor, has no resale value and requires proper disposal through your licensed hauler. Keeping these two streams separate, rather than letting cooking oil end up in the trap unnecessarily, both reduces your pumping frequency and can turn a portion of your kitchen waste into a modest revenue line instead of a pure expense.

How Kitchen Layout Affects Compliance

Where a grease trap sits in relation to your kitchen's fixtures matters more than many new restaurant owners realize during buildout. An interceptor installed too far from the fixtures generating the most FOG, typically the dish pit and fryer stations, gives grease more distance to cool and solidify in the connecting pipe before it ever reaches the trap. That buildup inside the pipe itself, separate from the trap, is a common cause of slow drains that a grease trap pumping alone won't fix, since the problem sits upstream of the trap rather than inside it.

FAQs

Does every restaurant need a grease trap?

Most food service establishments do, though the exact threshold and requirements vary by jurisdiction. Confirming with your local water authority is the reliable way to know for certain.

How often should a grease trap be pumped?

It depends on kitchen volume and trap size, but many commercial kitchens land on a monthly to quarterly schedule. Your local requirements may specify a minimum frequency.

Can hydro jetting replace grease trap pumping?

No, they serve different purposes. Pumping removes accumulated grease from the trap itself, while jetting clears buildup from the connected pipes.

What records should I keep for an inspection?

Pumping manifests from your licensed hauler, along with any internal maintenance logs, are typically what an inspector will ask to see.

Can a grease trap violation affect our lease or other permits?

In some cases, yes, particularly if a violation results in a suspended discharge permit or a health department action tied to the same address.
