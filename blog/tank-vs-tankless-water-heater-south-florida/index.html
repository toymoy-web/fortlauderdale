Tank vs. Tankless Water Heaters: What Actually Makes Sense Here

The efficiency numbers are only part of the decision. Here's what South Florida's water and climate add to the equation.

Published September 2026.

Replacing a failed water heater is a decision most homeowners have to make with little warning. It's worth knowing the trade-offs before the choice gets made for you at the worst possible moment, usually a cold shower on a Monday morning. In South Florida's climate and with its particular water quality, the tank-versus-tankless decision has a few local wrinkles worth understanding.

The Efficiency Numbers, According To The Department Of Energy

The U.S. Department of Energy estimates that tankless water heaters can be 24 to 34 percent more energy-efficient than a standard storage tank model for households using 41 gallons or less of hot water daily. For higher-usage households, around 86 gallons a day, the efficiency advantage narrows to roughly 8 to 14 percent. The difference comes down to standby heat loss: a tank keeps a large volume of water hot around the clock, while a tankless unit only heats water as it's actually being used.

Lifespan Is Part Of The Math Too

Tank Water Heaters

Typically last 10 to 15 years, sometimes less in a coastal, humid climate like Fort Lauderdale's.

Tankless Water Heaters

Generally rated for 20 or more years of service life with proper maintenance.

Upfront Cost

Tankless units cost more to purchase and install, often requiring venting or gas line changes, typically $2,500 to $4,500 installed versus $1,400 to $2,000 for a standard tank.

Long-Term Cost

The efficiency gain and longer lifespan can offset the higher upfront cost over the unit's lifetime, particularly for lower hot-water-use households.

At A Glance

Which One Actually Fits Your Household

Choose Tank If

  • Your household has heavy, simultaneous hot water use across multiple bathrooms.
  • Upfront budget is the deciding factor over the next decade of operating cost.
  • Your existing gas line and venting already support the current setup.

Choose Tankless If

  • Your household uses 41 gallons or less of hot water daily, where the efficiency gain is largest.
  • You plan to stay in the home long enough to recoup the higher upfront cost.
  • You're comfortable maintaining an annual descaling schedule for South Florida's water.

Rebates And Incentives Worth Checking

Federal and utility-level incentives for high-efficiency water heaters change from year to year, and a tankless unit sometimes qualifies for a rebate or tax credit that a standard tank replacement doesn't. Checking current programs before purchasing can meaningfully offset the higher upfront cost of a tankless conversion. These programs vary by utility provider and change periodically, so confirming current availability at the time of your purchase is worth the few extra minutes it takes.

It's worth asking specifically rather than assuming a rebate applies automatically. Some programs require a specific efficiency rating or a particular installation method to qualify, and a technician familiar with current program requirements can confirm whether your chosen unit actually meets them before you buy.

Why South Florida's Water Adds A Wrinkle

Mineral content in South Florida's municipal water builds scale inside a tankless unit's heat exchanger faster than it would in an area with naturally softer water. Most manufacturers recommend annual descaling for tankless units in hard water regions, and skipping that maintenance step can both reduce efficiency and, in some cases, void warranty coverage. This doesn't rule out tankless as an option here; it just means the maintenance schedule matters more than it would somewhere with softer water.

Salt air and coastal humidity are a separate factor that affects tank units specifically, since exposed metal fittings and tank exteriors corrode faster near the coast and canal system than they would inland. That's part of why a tank water heater in a Fort Lauderdale beachfront condo sometimes needs replacement sooner than the same model would need inland.

What A Household's Peak Demand Actually Looks Like

Sizing a tankless unit correctly means thinking about peak simultaneous demand, not just average daily use. A single shower running alongside a dishwasher and a washing machine on hot water represents a very different flow-rate requirement than one shower running alone. Tankless units are rated by maximum flow at a given temperature rise, and undersizing for your household's actual peak use is the most common reason a homeowner ends up disappointed with a tankless conversion. A proper sizing assessment accounts for your specific fixture count and household habits rather than applying a generic square-footage rule, and it's worth asking a technician to walk through that math with you before committing to a specific unit.

Which Makes Sense For Your Home

Estimate Daily Hot Water Use

A single person or couple typically uses less than a large family running multiple showers and a dishwasher.

Check Your Gas And Venting Setup

Converting to tankless sometimes requires upgrading a gas line or adding proper venting.

Factor In How Long You'll Stay

A longer lifespan matters more if you plan to be in the home for many years.

Get A Professional Sizing Assessment

The right unit size depends on your specific household, not a generic recommendation.

FAQs

Can I just swap my tank for a tankless in the same spot?

Sometimes, but tankless units often have different venting and gas supply requirements, which is worth assessing before assuming a direct swap.

Do tankless units really provide endless hot water?

Yes, in the sense that they don't run out the way a tank can, though they are flow-rate limited, so multiple simultaneous high-demand fixtures can exceed a single unit's output.

Is tankless worth it for a smaller household?

Often yes, since the efficiency gain is largest for households using less hot water daily, per the Department of Energy's figures.

What happens to my old tank when I switch to tankless?

Removal and disposal of the old unit is typically included as part of a professional installation, so you don't have to arrange separate disposal.

Does a tankless unit need a bigger gas line?

Sometimes, since tankless units often draw more gas at peak output than a standard tank. A technician will check your existing line's capacity before installation.
