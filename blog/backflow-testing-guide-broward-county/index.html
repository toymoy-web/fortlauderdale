A Homeowner's Guide to Backflow Testing in Broward County

Backflow testing is required for a reason. Here's what the device does, who needs annual testing, and what happens if you skip it.

Backflow testing shows up on a lot of Fort Lauderdale homeowners' and business owners' to-do lists once a year, often as a compliance requirement rather than a choice. Here's what the testing actually protects, who needs it, what the process involves, and what a lapsed test can cost you beyond the obvious inconvenience.

What Backflow Prevention Actually Does

A backflow preventer stops water from flowing in reverse through a plumbing connection. This matters because a sudden drop in municipal water pressure, say during a water main break or heavy firefighting demand nearby, can otherwise pull contaminated water back into the clean water supply. Irrigation systems, pools, and fire sprinkler systems are common connection points where this risk exists, since they involve water that could carry fertilizer, chemicals, or standing-water contaminants back toward the drinking water system if pressure reversed unexpectedly.

The Standards Behind The Device

ASSE International, accredited by the American National Standards Institute, publishes the product performance standards that govern how backflow prevention devices are designed and certified. Those standards are referenced directly by the plumbing codes that require testing in the first place. A separate ASSE standard series governs who is qualified to test and repair these devices, which is why testing has to be performed by a certified tester rather than done as a DIY project.

Different Devices For Different Hazard Levels

Not every connection carries the same contamination risk, and backflow devices are categorized accordingly. A simple hose bib vacuum breaker protects a low-hazard connection like an outdoor spigot, while a reduced pressure zone assembly, generally the highest-protection category, is typically required for a high-hazard connection like a commercial kitchen or an irrigation system that uses reclaimed water. Choosing the correct device type for a given connection isn't optional. It's determined by the hazard level the connection presents, which a licensed plumber assesses before recommending or installing a specific assembly.

Who Actually Needs Testing

Homes With Irrigation Systems

A residential irrigation system typically has its own dedicated backflow preventer subject to annual testing requirements.

Commercial Properties

Most commercial properties are required to test backflow assemblies annually to maintain uninterrupted water service.

Pool Fill Lines

A dedicated backflow device is often required where a pool fill line connects directly to the potable water supply.

Fire Sprinkler Systems

Fire suppression systems typically have their own dedicated backflow preventer subject to the same annual testing requirement.

What Happens During A Test

Visual Inspection

The device is checked for visible wear, corrosion, or physical damage before testing begins.

Pressure Testing

Specialized gauges test whether the device's internal check valves are holding pressure properly.

Documentation

Results are recorded and filed with the required utility or municipal authority.

Repair If Failed

A device that fails testing is repaired or fully replaced, then retested before the process is considered complete.

What To Do If You Suspect A Backflow Failure

Watch For Unusual Taste, Odor, Or Color

A sudden change in your water's taste, smell, or appearance can be an early sign of a cross-connection issue and is worth reporting promptly.

Shut Off The Suspect Connection

If you can safely do so, shutting off the irrigation or connection you suspect is affected limits further risk while you wait for a technician.

Avoid Drinking Water Until Checked

If you notice discoloration or an unusual odor, use bottled or a known-safe source until a plumber has assessed the situation.

Call A Certified Tester

Only a certified backflow tester should assess and repair a suspected failure. Don't attempt to open or adjust the device yourself.

What Happens If You Skip It

Missing an annual test isn't just a paperwork issue. In many Broward County jurisdictions, a lapsed backflow test can result in water service being restricted or shut off until compliance is restored, which is a far more disruptive outcome than the test itself. For a commercial property, it can also mean a failed inspection that affects other permits or renewals tied to the same address.

Canal-Front Properties Carry Extra Risk

Fort Lauderdale's residential canal network adds a genuine wrinkle for properties that draw irrigation water from the canal itself, alongside a municipal connection. That dual-source setup carries a meaningfully higher cross-connection risk than a property irrigated entirely from municipal water, since canal water is untreated and can carry contaminants a standard backyard irrigation system was never designed to filter out. If your property has this kind of dual setup, confirming your backflow device is rated for that specific configuration is worth a direct conversation with a licensed plumber, not an assumption based on a standard residential install.

FAQs

How long does a backflow test take?

Most residential tests take well under an hour to complete. Commercial properties with multiple devices take longer depending on how many assemblies need testing.

Can I test my own backflow device?

No, testing must be performed and certified by someone qualified under the applicable ASSE standard, so a DIY test doesn't satisfy the requirement.

What if my device fails the test?

It can typically be repaired or replaced the same visit and retested before the technician leaves.

Do I need a permit to install a new backflow device?

In most jurisdictions, yes. A licensed plumber handles the permit process as part of installing a new device.

Does a backflow device ever need to be replaced, not just tested?

Yes, internal components wear out over years of use, and a device that repeatedly fails testing eventually makes more sense to replace than to keep repairing.
