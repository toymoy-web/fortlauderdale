Pool Leak Vs. Structural Leak: How To Tell The Difference

Most pool water loss is a plumbing issue, not a cracked shell. Here's how to test for the difference before assuming the worst.

A pool losing water faster than expected sends a lot of Fort Lauderdale homeowners down a worrying path, wondering if the pool shell itself is cracking and facing a costly resurfacing project. Most of the time, the actual cause is far less dramatic: a plumbing leak in the lines running to and from the pool, not the structure itself. Here's how to tell the difference before assuming the worst.

Start With The Bucket Test

Before assuming any kind of leak, rule out ordinary evaporation first. Place a bucket on a pool step, filled to match the pool's water level, and mark both levels clearly. Check again after 24 hours. If the pool has dropped noticeably more than the bucket, which reflects evaporation alone, you likely have an actual leak rather than just a hot, dry stretch of weather.

Signs That Point To A Plumbing Leak

Wet Or Soggy Equipment Pad

A consistently damp area around the pump, filter, or heater often points to a leak at that equipment, not the shell.

Air Bubbling From Return Lines

Air in the lines suggests a suction-side leak drawing air in along with water.

Pump Struggles To Prime

A suction leak can make the pump work harder or fail to build proper suction.

Water Loss Only While The Pump Runs

If the pool only loses water when the pump is on, a pressure-side plumbing leak is more likely than a shell issue.

Signs That Point To A Structural Issue

Cracks Visible In The Shell

Visible cracking, especially with water seeping directly from it, points to structural damage rather than a plumbing line.

Water Loss Regardless Of Pump Status

Consistent loss whether the pump is running or not can suggest a shell-level issue rather than an isolated plumbing line.

Settling Or Shifting Deck

Ground movement affecting the surrounding deck alongside water loss can indicate a structural problem.

Loss Concentrated At A Specific Wall Or Floor Area

A localized wet spot outside the pool near one section of the shell suggests a structural leak at that location.

What Happens If A Leak Goes Unaddressed

Beyond the obvious cost of refilling a pool repeatedly, an ongoing plumbing leak near the equipment pad or return lines can erode surrounding soil over time. That erosion can eventually affect decking or even nearby structures if it continues long enough without repair. A structural shell leak carries its own separate risk of worsening cracks as water continues to find its way through weakened concrete or gunite. Neither type of leak tends to improve on its own, which is part of why an accurate diagnosis followed by a timely repair, rather than repeatedly topping off the water level, is the more cost-effective long-term approach for any pool owner.

Why This Distinction Matters For Repair Cost

A plumbing leak is generally a more contained, less expensive repair, whether that means fixing a cracked skimmer line, tightening or replacing an equipment pad fitting, or repairing a return line. A structural shell issue is a different specialty altogether, typically handled by a pool contractor rather than a plumber, and usually a larger repair project. Getting the diagnosis right before starting any repair avoids paying for the wrong kind of work entirely.

Where Pool Plumbing Leaks Actually Happen

When the diagnosis does point to plumbing, the leak location tends to cluster in a few predictable spots. Skimmer line connections can crack from ground settling in Fort Lauderdale's sandy soil, which shifts more than the denser clay-based soil found in some other regions. Equipment pad fittings loosen over years of vibration and pressure cycling. Return lines running from the equipment pad to the pool itself can develop cracks from the same ground movement affecting skimmer lines. Knowing these common locations helps a technician narrow the search faster than starting from scratch on an unfamiliar system.

How We Confirm Which One You Have

Pressure testing the pool's plumbing lines in isolation from the shell is the reliable way to separate the two, similar in principle to how our general leak detection process isolates sections of a home's plumbing. If the lines hold pressure independently and the leak persists, that points toward the shell rather than the plumbing, and we'll say so plainly rather than attempting a plumbing fix that won't solve a structural problem. If the lines don't hold pressure, we've found the actual source and can move directly to repair, following the plumbing provisions of the Florida Building Code, based on the International Plumbing Code (IPC).

FAQs

How much water loss is normal from evaporation?

Roughly a quarter to half an inch per day is common in Fort Lauderdale's climate, more during hot, windy, or dry stretches.

Can a plumbing leak near the pool affect my foundation?

A significant, ongoing leak near a home's foundation can contribute to soil erosion issues over time, which is part of why prompt repair matters.

Do you handle structural pool repairs too?

No, we handle the plumbing side specifically. A confirmed structural issue would go to a pool contractor for that specialized repair.

Is it possible to have both a plumbing and a structural leak at once?

Yes, though it's less common in practice. Testing the plumbing in isolation is the reliable way to know whether one, both, or neither is actually contributing to the water loss you're seeing.

Does salt water affect the likelihood of plumbing leaks?

Saltwater pool systems don't inherently cause more plumbing leaks than chlorine systems, but certain fittings and equipment need to be rated for saltwater use to avoid accelerated corrosion over time and eventual failure.
